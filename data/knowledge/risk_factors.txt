Several interrelated biological, mechanical, and behavioral factors contribute to the onset and progression of temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJ-OA). These risk factors influence both the structural integrity of the joint and its ability to withstand functional stress over time.

Age is one of the most significant determinants of TMJ degeneration. With advancing age, there is a gradual decline in cartilage regenerative capacity, reduction in collagen quality, and increased susceptibility to microstructural damage. Age-related changes in subchondral bone, including reduced bone density and altered remodeling patterns, further compromise joint stability. As a result, older individuals are more prone to progressive cartilage breakdown and degenerative bone changes within the temporomandibular joint.

Mechanical loading plays a crucial role in TMJ health. Repetitive stress caused by parafunctional habits such as bruxism (teeth grinding), jaw clenching, excessive chewing, and abnormal occlusion leads to increased joint loading and uneven force distribution. Clinical symptoms such as jaw clicking and grinding sounds (crepitus) often reflect internal derangements, disc displacement, or surface irregularities in the joint. Persistent mechanical stress accelerates cartilage wear, promotes subchondral bone remodeling, and contributes to progressive joint degeneration.

Pain intensity is closely associated with both inflammatory processes and structural damage within the joint. Increased pain levels often indicate active disease progression, synovial inflammation, and heightened joint sensitivity. Chronic pain may also lead to altered jaw movement patterns, further exacerbating mechanical stress and degeneration.

Inflammatory processes play a central role in TMJ-OA progression. The release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and mediators contributes to cartilage degradation, bone resorption, and joint tissue damage. This inflammatory environment can accelerate disease progression and amplify clinical symptoms.

In addition to these factors, structural abnormalities such as changes in bone volume fraction (BVTV), trabecular architecture, and condylar morphology are strongly linked to disease risk. Reduced bone density and disrupted microarchitecture weaken the joint’s ability to absorb mechanical loads, making it more vulnerable to degeneration.

The interaction between age-related degeneration, mechanical stress, inflammation, and structural bone changes determines the overall risk and progression rate of TMJ osteoarthritis. Understanding these combined factors is essential for early detection, risk stratification, and personalized management of the condition.
