Temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJ-OA) is a progressive degenerative condition characterized by continuous structural and functional deterioration of the joint over time. The progression of the disease involves complex interactions between cartilage degradation, subchondral bone remodeling, mechanical stress, and inflammatory processes.

In the early stage of TMJ osteoarthritis, structural changes are subtle and may not be clearly visible on conventional imaging. Patients may experience mild discomfort, occasional joint clicking, and slight instability of the joint. At this stage, initial alterations in cartilage composition and early microstructural changes in trabecular bone, such as slight reductions in Bone Volume Fraction (BVTV), may already be present, even before significant clinical symptoms appear.

During the intermediate stage, disease progression becomes more evident. There is increased cartilage thinning, active bone remodeling, and the onset of inflammatory responses within the joint. Patients often report persistent pain, stiffness, and more frequent joint sounds such as clicking or grinding (crepitus). Radiographic and radiomic findings may reveal noticeable changes in trabecular architecture, including decreased BVTV, reduced trabecular number, and increased trabecular separation, all of which indicate weakening of the bone structure.

In advanced stages, TMJ osteoarthritis is characterized by severe cartilage loss, significant subchondral bone erosion, osteophyte formation, and deformation of the mandibular condyle. Joint function becomes markedly impaired, leading to restricted jaw movement, chronic pain, and reduced quality of life. At this stage, structural damage is often irreversible, and the joint exhibits poor load-bearing capacity.

The rate of disease progression varies among individuals and is influenced by multiple factors, including age, mechanical loading, bone density, and overall joint health. Increased mechanical stress due to bruxism, jaw clenching, or abnormal occlusion accelerates degeneration by causing repetitive microtrauma. Reduced bone density and declining BVTV further compromise structural integrity, making the joint more susceptible to rapid deterioration.

Longitudinal changes in radiomic features, particularly BVTV_change between baseline and follow-up measurements, serve as important indicators of disease progression. A continuous decrease in BVTV over time reflects ongoing bone resorption and worsening joint condition, whereas stable values may indicate slower progression.

Understanding the temporal progression of TMJ osteoarthritis is essential for risk stratification, early intervention, and treatment planning. By integrating clinical symptoms, radiomic features, and progression patterns, it becomes possible to predict patient outcomes more accurately and guide personalized management strategies.
